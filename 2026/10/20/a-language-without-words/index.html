Raw materials · 2 min read

A language without words.

October 20, 2026

Iris is the most patient material in perfumery. The scent does not live in the violet petals at all, but in the root — the rhizome — and it does not appear until years after the harvest.

Three years of waiting

The rhizomes of Iris pallida are lifted from the earth, peeled by hand, and left to rest in dark, dry rooms for as long as three years. Slowly, almost invisibly, the molecules responsible for that famous powdery softness develop. Only then are the roots distilled into orris butter — one of the most precious materials a perfumer can work with.

It takes roughly a tonne of dried roots to produce a couple of kilograms of orris butter. You can understand, then, why it is used with care.

Iris smells the way a cashmere sweater feels: cool at first touch, then warm against the skin.

What it brings to Fleur de Peau

In Fleur de Peau, iris sits at the heart, between a bright breath of orange blossom and a cloud of white musk. It is not loud. It does not announce itself. Instead it gives the composition a texture — something soft, slightly earthy, faintly like violets and clean skin — that lingers long after the brighter notes have gone.

  • Opening — orange blossom, a hint of pear
  • Heart — orris butter, iris absolute
  • Base — white musk, ambrette seed

If you have ever found a fragrance described as “powdery” and wondered what that means, try Fleur de Peau on your wrist and wait twenty minutes. That quiet, velvety hum is iris, speaking in the only language it knows.
